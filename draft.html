GEOG 560, Fall 2024, Oregon State University

My Research Interests

My research interests lie at the intersection of geospatial science, data analysis, and technology. I am particularly fascinated by the use of GIS, open-source tools, and advanced computational methods to analyze and model spatial data. My focus includes exploring innovative approaches to data visualization and interpretation, integrating AI and machine learning for geospatial applications, and addressing the human and societal dimensions of spatial data.

I aim to contribute to projects that merge my engineering background with coding skills to solve complex, real-world problems. Specifically, I am interested in projects that enhance decision-making, improve spatial understanding, and promote open knowledge initiatives. My goal is to advance methodologies that make geospatial analysis more accessible, scalable, and impactful, both for academic research and practical applications.

Annotations

Anderson, B. (2016). Cultural geography 1: Intensities and forms of power. Progress in Human Geography, 41(4), 501–511. https://doi.org/10.1177/0309132516649491
This paper provides a critical review of cultural geography’s exploration of power, focusing on its intensities and forms in contemporary contexts. Anderson examines how cultural geographers address issues such as precaritization, dispossession, and anti-black violence, highlighting the lived experiences of power dynamics. The article introduces a “politics of experience,” emphasizing how power shapes and is shaped by human and non-human interactions. This work is particularly relevant to my research interests as it bridges theoretical frameworks with practical implications, offering insights into the role of spatial data and GIS in understanding power relations and fostering more equitable spatial practices.
Black, B. (2024). Remote Sensing-Based Coastal Environmental Monitoring Approaches for Remote Island Settings. Oregon State University. https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/concern/graduate_thesis_or_dissertations/s1784v71h
This thesis explores the application of remote sensing technologies for monitoring coastal environments, focusing on the unique challenges of remote island settings. Black emphasizes the integration of satellite imagery, drone surveys, and machine learning algorithms to address issues such as habitat degradation, shoreline changes, and resource management. The study’s findings underscore the importance of high-resolution imagery and tailored algorithms for accurate environmental monitoring in isolated areas. This research is highly relevant to my interests in applying geospatial science to environmental challenges, as it demonstrates practical methodologies for integrating data analysis and AI in resource-scarce settings. It also provides valuable insights into using open-source tools for spatial analysis in remote regions.
Buzai, G. D., & Montes-Galbán, E. (2021). Estadística espacial: Fundamentos y aplicación con sistemas de información geográfica. Buenos Aires: Impresiones Buenos Aires Editorial.
This book provides a comprehensive foundation for spatial statistics and its applications through Geographic Information Systems (GIS). It explores the evolution of spatial analysis, integrating theoretical insights and practical methodologies for analyzing and interpreting spatial data. The authors cover central topics such as spatial distribution, autocorrelation, interpolation techniques, and accessibility modeling, illustrated with case studies and practical exercises using QGIS. This resource is highly relevant to my research interests, as it offers a blend of quantitative methods and GIS tools to tackle spatial problems effectively. Its emphasis on open-source applications and real-world examples resonates with my focus on accessible, data-driven approaches for geospatial analysis and decision-making.
Cresswell, T. (2013). Geographic Thought: A Critical Introduction. Wiley-Blackwell.
This book provides a detailed exploration of the theoretical and philosophical developments that have shaped geography, with a focus on the quantitative revolution and its impact on spatial science. Chapter 5 discusses the historical context and philosophical underpinnings of the shift towards mathematical and generalizable models in geography, emphasizing the influence of positivism. Cresswell critically examines how the pursuit of scientific rigor redefined geographic inquiry, fostering methods that integrated quantitative analysis and spatial modeling. This work is particularly relevant to my research interests as it contextualizes the evolution of GIS and highlights the balance between quantitative methods and human-centered approaches, offering insights into the philosophical debates that underpin modern geospatial science.
De Stefano, L., Petersen-Perlman, J. D., Sproles, E. A., Eynard, J., & Wolf, A. T. (2017). Assessment of transboundary river basins for potential hydro-political tensions. Global Environmental Change, 45, 35–46. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2017.04.008
This paper systematically evaluates transboundary river basins globally to identify regions at risk of hydro-political tensions, integrating environmental, political, and economic indicators. The authors emphasize the challenges posed by new water infrastructure, such as dams, in basins with insufficient institutional capacity to manage tensions. Key findings highlight areas in Africa, Asia, and South America where rapid infrastructure development coincides with weak transboundary agreements, heightening the risk of conflict. This study is crucial for my research on using geospatial data and institutional analysis to predict and mitigate resource-related disputes. Its methodology for mapping risks aligns with my interest in employing data-driven approaches to support policy and capacity-building efforts in conflict-prone areas.
DeLyser, D., & Sui, D. (2012). Crossing the qualitative-quantitative divide II: Inventive approaches to big data, mobile methods, and rhythmanalysis. Progress in Human Geography, 37(2), 293–305. https://doi.org/10.1177/0309132512444063
This article explores innovative methodological approaches bridging qualitative and quantitative geography, focusing on the implications of big data, mobile methods, and rhythmanalysis. The authors highlight the growing synergy between digital humanities and geospatial analysis, showcasing how these methodologies enable new insights into complex geographic phenomena. By emphasizing the importance of interdisciplinary and inventive approaches, the study aligns with my research interests in leveraging GIS and AI to address contemporary challenges. Its discussion on mobile methods and rhythmanalysis offers valuable frameworks for integrating human experiences and spatial data in environmental and social research contexts.
DeLyser, D., & Sui, D. (2013). Crossing the qualitative-quantitative chasm III: Enduring methods, open geography, participatory research, and the fourth paradigm. Progress in Human Geography, 38(2), 294–307. https://doi.org/10.1177/0309132513479291
This article explores methodological pluralism in geography, emphasizing the integration of enduring and emerging methods to bridge the qualitative-quantitative divide. It advocates for open geography, participatory research, and the adoption of the “fourth paradigm” to address complex societal challenges. The authors highlight the value of traditional approaches like mapping and interviewing, alongside data-intensive methodologies, to foster inclusive and impactful geographic research. This work aligns with my interest in blending GIS, participatory methods, and open science principles to advance geospatial understanding and problem-solving, especially in areas where methodological diversity enhances the representation of human and environmental systems.
Duggan, M. (2024). All mapped out: How maps shape us. Reaktion Books, Ltd. https://public.ebookcentral.proquest.com/choice/PublicFullRecord.aspx?p=31131781
This book explores the profound impact of maps on society, culture, and economies, spanning from ancient cartographic practices to modern digital mapping technologies. Duggan highlights the dual role of maps as tools for navigation and instruments for shaping personal and collective identities. Through historical and contemporary examples, the author reveals how mapping technologies influence the way we perceive and interact with the world. This work aligns closely with my research interests in GIS, spatial data, and the societal dimensions of mapping. Its focus on the interplay between geography, data, and people provides valuable insights into the cultural and political implications of cartographic practices, supporting my exploration of maps as powerful mediums for both representation and transformation.
Esson, J., Noxolo, P., Baxter, R., Daley, P., & Byron, M. (2017). The 2017 RGS-IBG chair’s theme: Decolonising geographical knowledges, or reproducing coloniality? Area, 49(3), 384–388. https://doi.org/10.1111/area.12371
This commentary critiques the 2017 Royal Geographical Society’s conference theme, “Decolonising Geographical Knowledges,” arguing that the focus on knowledge production risks perpetuating coloniality if not accompanied by structural and institutional reform. The authors advocate for decolonial practices led by marginalized voices, emphasizing the intersectionality of race and indigeneity. This work is relevant to my interests in understanding power dynamics within geography and exploring how GIS and spatial data can challenge colonial legacies. It offers a critical framework for considering the role of knowledge production in fostering equitable spatial practices.
Fleishman, E., & Brown, H. (2019). Use of macroecology to integrate social justice and conservation. Global Ecology and Biogeography, 28(12), 1512–1518. https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12965
This paper explores the integration of macroecology with social justice and conservation biology, emphasizing the role of spatial and temporal analyses in addressing equity and sustainability. The authors illustrate how macroecological methods can inform policies on climate change, health disparities, and environmental protection, linking human and ecological systems. The study also highlights the disproportionate impact of environmental changes on marginalized communities and the need for participatory conservation approaches. This work is highly relevant to my research on the human dimensions of geospatial science and AI, offering a framework for incorporating social justice metrics into environmental data models and decision-making.

Key findings reveal that climate-induced risks are intensifying, particularly for vulnerable populations and ecosystems. The report identifies critical adaptation and mitigation strategies and underscores the importance of climate-resilient development. This document is instrumental for my research as it integrates social, ecological, and technological dimensions, aligning with my interest in using GIS and data-driven approaches to address complex, multidisciplinary challenges posed by climate change.
Kidd, C., Levizzani, V., & Bauer, P. (2009). A review of satellite meteorology and climatology at the start of the twenty-first century. Progress in Physical Geography, 33(4), 474–489. https://doi.org/10.1177/0309133309346647
This article provides an extensive overview of the advancements in satellite meteorology and climatology, focusing on how satellite data has revolutionized our understanding of atmospheric processes. The authors review key technologies such as geostationary and polar-orbiting satellites and their applications in weather prediction, climate monitoring, and hydrometeorology. They highlight the role of satellite data in improving numerical weather prediction models and monitoring the impacts of climate change on global scales. This work is highly relevant to my research, as it demonstrates the integration of remote sensing and data assimilation techniques, offering valuable methodologies for geospatial analysis in environmental science.
Mendenhall, E., Hendrix, C., Nyman, E., Roberts, P. M., Hoopes, J. R., Watson, J. R., Lam, V. W. Y., & Sumaila, R. (2020). Climate change increases the risk of fisheries conflict. Marine Policy, 117, 103954. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpol.2020.103954
This article examines the link between climate change and the potential for conflict in fisheries, highlighting how shifting marine ecosystems and resource availability exacerbate geopolitical tensions. The authors use interdisciplinary approaches to analyze case studies and predictive models, showing that warming oceans, changing fish distributions, and resource scarcity are primary drivers of conflict risk. This study is particularly valuable for my research interests as it demonstrates the importance of integrating geospatial data and environmental variables to understand and mitigate human-environment conflicts. Its focus on predictive modeling aligns with my goals of exploring AI and data analysis for resource management and conflict prevention.
Overture Maps Foundation. (n.d.). Home. Retrieved December 3, 2024, from https://overturemaps.org/
The Overture Maps Foundation is a collaborative initiative founded in December 2022 by Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and TomTom, operating under the Linux Foundation. Its mission is to develop reliable, easy-to-use, and interoperable open map data to support current and future mapping applications. By integrating data from various sources—including member contributions, civic organizations, and open data—the foundation aims to create a comprehensive mapping ecosystem. Key features include a Global Entity Reference System to unify disparate datasets, rigorous quality assurance processes to maintain data integrity, and the establishment of a structured data schema to facilitate the development of map products. This initiative is pertinent to my research interests in GIS, AI, and open knowledge, as it exemplifies a concerted effort to democratize access to high-quality geospatial data, fostering innovation and collaboration across the geospatial community.

Woodill, A. J., Kavanaugh, M., Harte, M., & Watson, J. R. (2021). Ocean seascapes predict distant-water fishing vessel incursions into exclusive economic zones. Fish and Fisheries, 22(5), 892-907. https://doi.org/10.1111/faf.12559
This study uses machine learning and geospatial data to predict incursions of distant-water fishing vessels into exclusive economic zones (EEZs), focusing on the Argentine Patagonian Shelf. The research leverages oceanographic variables, such as sea surface temperature and chlorophyll concentration, to create predictive models that identify patterns of illegal fishing with up to 96% accuracy. The authors emphasize the use of seascapes—dynamic oceanographic categorizations—as critical predictors. This paper is particularly relevant to my research interests in using GIS and AI for environmental monitoring and decision support. It demonstrates how spatial data and predictive modeling can enhance monitoring strategies and inform policy, aligning with my focus on integrating open-source geospatial tools for resource management and governance.
Yin, D., & He, F. (2014). A simple method for estimating species abundance from occurrence maps. Methods in Ecology and Evolution, 5(4), 336–343. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12159
This study introduces a novel, data-efficient method for estimating species abundance using occurrence maps, focusing on spatial autocorrelation as a critical factor. The proposed method outperforms traditional models like random placement and negative binomial distribution by addressing aggregation in species distribution. The authors validate their approach using both simulated and empirical data, highlighting its effectiveness and low data requirements compared to other methods. This work is particularly relevant to my research interests, as it demonstrates how GIS and spatial analysis can enhance ecological modeling and conservation planning, offering practical solutions for resource-limited scenarios.
